DROUGHT
01

Drought has always been natural.

Every continent has experienced drought since long before industrialisation. The Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) measures how wet or dry a region is relative to its own historical average — zero is normal, negative is drier, positive is wetter.

But over 160 years of climate records, the geography of drought is shifting. The question is not whether drought exists — it is where it is going, and how fast.

South America

The Clearest Signal

drying

South America is the only continent that is simultaneously drying in mean SPI, increasing in drought frequency, and continuing that trend over time.

The Brazilian cerrado and Argentine pampas — among the world's most productive agricultural zones — sit at the centre of this signal. When the drought pattern that farmers planned for is no longer the drought they face, adaptation becomes nearly impossible.

"The risk is not just drought. It is the disappearance of predictability."

Africa's drying signal is more modest than South America's in magnitude, but the human stakes are disproportionately high. Much of sub-Saharan agriculture is rain-fed and operates with minimal buffer against seasonal disruption.

Even a small upward shift in drought frequency — 2.9 percentage points — translates into meaningfully more years where crops fail and food systems come under stress.

Asia

Wetter, But Not Safer

+0.24 SPI change
wetting
+0.98pp Variability
increase

Asia's average SPI has increased substantially in the modern period — wetter overall. Yet variability has also increased, meaning the swings between wet and dry years are growing wider.

Agriculture needs predictability more than it needs rainfall. A wetter average with higher variability can still devastate crop calendars calibrated to expected seasonal patterns.

04

What the data supports

South America and Africa show consistent drying signals and rising drought frequency — the clearest threat to agricultural stability.

Asia, Europe, and Antarctica are trending wetter on average, but several show increasing variability — instability by another name.

The dominant pattern is not global drying. It is regional divergence — the redistribution of drought risk across a warming planet.
